## December 22, 2015

## Summary of the Electronic Consultation with the Technical Advisory Committee on Monetary Policy: November 2015

Consultation with external Members of the Technical Advisory Committee (TAC) on Monetary Policy was held electronically between November 20 and 24, 2015 in the run up to the <u>Fifth Bi-monthly Monetary Policy Review, 2015-16 on December 1, 2015</u>. The main points suggested by Members are set out below.

1. Members expressed concern that the global demand is still weak. The coming month is likely to see divergence in policies of major central banks. The Federal Reserve is likely to announce a rate increase in December as the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) grows confident that low inflation will rebound and employment will move below the natural rate. At this juncture, it is unlikely that US growth will slow below trend. With US GDP approximating 2.1 per cent in Q3 of 2015, actual growth may even exceed estimates of US potential growth suggesting impending price pressures. Given this, the FOMC may feel that it may be appropriate to initiate the normalization process in the December meeting. Other central banks however have not been particularly successful to pull off the zero lower bound – the European Central Bank is likely to expand asset purchases and possibly further cut its already negative deposit rate, Bank of Japan witnessing domestic recession will also amplify its expansionary policy, and the People’s Bank of China will attempt to add stimulus to the economy as well.
2. On domestic growth, pending the GDP release on November 30, Members felt that demand, as also credit growth continue to be weak with no significant change since the last policy announcement. The reduction in area sown under *rabi* along with the marginal increase in *kharif* production indicates agricultural output is either stable or declining this year. A small improvement in manufacturing, and muted overall positive change in service sector economic activity, indicate an economy that is still picking itself up. Indicators of investment demand pick up are scarce and the stress of bank balance sheets is still palpable. The most recent (September) IIP remains subdued, with only consumption of durables displaying robust growth. There is some growth in the capital goods sector reflecting the complementarity between public infrastructure capital and private investment. Given the complementarity, it is unlikely that private investment will take-off unless there is a sustained increase in public investment in the economy. There is also the issue of poor balance sheets of the corporate sector, especially in the infrastructure sector. The continuation of binding constraints to fresh capex spending would hinder private investment despite monetary policy being accommodative. Net exports, subdued because of secular stagnation and global slack, continue to pose downside risks to growth. Better revenue receipts garnered by the government is more due to increases in excise and service tax rates and cess than due to the underlying buoyancy of the economy. The recent improvement in the fiscal position could be negated by the implementation of One Rank One Pension and the Seventh Central Pay Commission (CPC) Award. The implementation of VII CPC proposals is likely to provide some demand stimulus for industry. If the government off-sets higher government consumption (on wages and pensions) through the VII CPC with lower

spending elsewhere, the inflationary impact may be limited. With growth slowing in the last quarter in the US and Euro area, India’s exports are likely to face a contraction. Members felt that in all likelihood there prevails a slim negative output gap. However, they opined, real sector policies are important to address this rather than monetary policy.

3. Members noted that inflation surprised on the upside with headline inflation rising sharply to 5 per cent in October, core CPI to 4.6 per cent, and ‘core-core’ (CPI excluding food, fuel, petrol and diesel) to 5.3 per cent. Food inflation also increased to 5.3 per cent in October with price pressures largely driven by pulses and onions. Notwithstanding the relative unreliability of survey-based inflation expectation measures (over market based ones), spike in pulses inflation of 42 per cent is likely to unmoor inflationary expectations making the downward movement of inflationary expectations unlikely in the short term. Volatility in specific components (like food) may make the anchoring of inflationary expectations challenging.
4. On external sector developments, Members expected currency to be volatile in the coming months due to sharp variations in monetary policy stances of major central banks around the world. With currency risk likely to become a large basis for turbulence, foreign exchange concerns will have a bearing on the investment climate and will require the focus to managing currency volatility. Inflation due to volatility in weather and seasonal disturbances may recede in importance relative to inflation due to external changes from exchange rates and asset prices.
5. Four of the five Members recommended status quo in the present policy. The reasons for a pause given by the Members were: first, headline CPI as well as measures of core inflation have risen sharply – while global commodity prices continue to impart some disinflationary momentum, there are some upside risks to inflation from ‘inflation-internals’ (negative monsoon shocks, binding supply side constraints, government consumption shocks such as the VII CPC). Firming up of inflation warrants monetary policy to be cautious as it keeps its immediate target of 6 per cent in its sight. Members felt that the rising inflation itself should warrant no change in financial accommodation. They were of the view that the bar for any further accommodation should increase as the Reserve Bank shifts towards inflation in the medium term. Second, with headline inflation at 5 per cent, the real policy repo rate was at 1.75 per cent. This is in the middle of the Reserve Bank’s preferred 1.5-2.0 per cent real neutral rate range, suggesting that policy is currently neutral. Third, there prevails a slim negative output gap that needs to be addressed by real sector policies rather than monetary policy. Fourth, since the last policy was frontloaded, it should be allowed to work through the system. Fifth, sharp variations in monetary policy stances of major central banks around the world in the coming month will lead to currency volatility. All these suggested a pause in any rate action by the Reserve Bank. On guidance, one of the Members recommended that the Reserve Bank should mention that the current accommodative stance will be data dependent (since data can surprise on both the upside and downside).
6. The fifth Member recommended a rate cut by 25 basis points since CPI inflation was low. The Member assumed that Central Government will meet its fiscal and revenue deficit targets in 2016-17.
7. All the five external Members – Dr. Shankar Acharya, Dr. Arvind Virmani, Prof. Errol D’Souza, Prof. Ashima Goyal, and Prof. Chetan Ghate – sent their feedback through e-mail. Since February 2011, the Reserve Bank has been placing main points of
discussions held in the meetings of TAC on Monetary Policy in the public domain with a lag of roughly four weeks after the meeting/consultation.
