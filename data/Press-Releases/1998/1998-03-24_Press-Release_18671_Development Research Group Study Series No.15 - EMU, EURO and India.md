Development Research Group Study Series No.15 : EMU, EURO and India

The Development Research Group (DRG) of the Reserve Bank of India has brought out

its Study Series No.15 entitled "EMU, EURO and India". The study is authored jointly by

Professor Ganti Subrahmanyam of the National Institute of Bank Management, Pune,

Economic Analysis and Policy of the Reserve Bank of India.

The study discusses a number ofissues which have assumed significance in the

international economy inview of the constitution of theEuropeanMonetary Union and

in the study include the desirability of EMU, problems relating to the transition to a single

currency EURO, the conduct of monetary policy by the European Central Bank (ECB),

the costs and benefits of a single currency, the role of EURO in the international

economy and the impact of EURO on the Indian economy.

Noting the presence of internal and external benefits of a single currency, the study

spells out several static and dynamic efficiency gains of a single currency. These

savings arising out of the exchange rate misalignments and the consequent

misallocation of resources, positive effects on trade and investment and the consequent

long-run positive response of productivity and efficiency gains due to the absence of

exchange rate risks. The sum of these gains is estimated to be around 15 per cent of

GDP. This apart, the EU will benefit from economies of scale, lower interest rates and a

stable financial environment. The study also highlights the costs in moving to one

money, including, the loss of earnings due to loss of spreads, floats, currency exchange

and cross border payments (estimated around 9.5 - 14.4 bn.ECUs) and the costs of

transition for banks and financial institutions in training, technological upgradation,

Moreover, the currency pattern of international bond, equity and derivative transactions suggest a crucial role of EURO as an investment currency while as a potential reserve currency it will have a second dominant position next only to the US dollar, given that the currency is strong and well managed. The study notes that while a single currency strengthens a single market, it implies loss of exchange rate as a policy instrument and seignorage revenue to the member countries and the loss of monetary policy as a notional policy instrument and the consequent reduced flexibility of fiscal policy.

The study also includes a detailed analysis of the impact of EURO on the Indian economy. Analysing the Indo-EU trading behaviour, the study notes the presence of several commercial policy constraints in the trading relationship between India and the EU. These include, the higher average tariff rates, presence of Quantitative Restrictions (QRs) such as quotas, various technical barriers in the form of product standards, certification requirements etc. and frequent investigations of anti-dumping complaints from the EU on Indian exporters. The study estimates that the creation of EURO will lead to a small loss in forex dealing to Indian banks while the loss of employment to the Indian banking sector will be negligible. Other costs to Indian banks will be minimal and relate mostly to training of staff, upgradation of information technology, marketing and legal issues etc. This apart, since the forex reserve management strategy is based on the safety considerations of hedging currency risk rather than expected return maximisation, the US dollar will remain the dominant currency in the forex portfolio in the immediate future.

The study observes that while official capital flows from EU to India is unlikely to improve due to the internal capital demands in EU countries for social programmes, the future of private capital inflows from EURO to India is promising in view of structural reforms which have significantly improved the investor-friendly environment in India. The Indian corporates will also benefit from increased access to the EU financial markets. However, the study highlights that there will be an increase in India's external debt if the US dollar depreciates at the time of EURO-unveiling while, a weak EURO will lead to decline of India's external debt in dollar term.

The DRG undertakes quick and effective policy-oriented research backed by strong analytical and empirical basis on subjects of current interest. DRG studies are the outcome of collaborative efforts between experts from outside the Reserve Bank and the pool of research talents within the Bank. These studies are released for wider circulation with a view to generating constructive discussion among professional economists and policy makers. The views expressed in these studies are those of the authors and do not reflect the views of the Reserve Bank.

## Cash Reserve Ratio

Under Section 42(1) of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, all scheduled commercial banks (excluding Regional Rural Banks) are, at present, required to maintain with the Reserve Bank of India, as cash reserve ratio (CRR) of 10.5 per cent of their net demand and time liabilities (excluding liabilities subject to zero CRR prescription). It has been decided to reduce the CRR. Accordingly, the CRR to be maintained by scheduled commercial banks (excluding Regional Rural Banks) is being reduced by 0.50 percentage point from 10.5 per cent to 10.0 per cent in two phases of 0.25 percentage point each as set out below:
