# Reserve Bank of India Occasional Papers-September 1998 Issue

## January 4, 1999

The September 1998 issue of the Reserve Bank of India Occasional Papers (OPs) contains three research articles, two notes, one review article and two book reviews. **They reflect the research** **efforts of the authors and their personal views.**

The first paper of the volume "Exchange Rate Management in India : An Empirical Evaluation" by Michael Debabrata Patra and Sitikantha Pattanaik attempts to develop objective indicators of exchange market pressure and intervention activity in India. The study is focussed on the recent exchange rate behaviour of the rupee, after it was left to the market in 1993. The study has developed an index of Exchange Market Pressure (EMP) and its operational variant, the Monetary Conditions Index (MCI), as a guide for exchange market intervention. Based on the empirical evidence, the paper shows that while interventions have been the principal operating variable they have been reinforced by monetary policy measures when the exchange market has been driven by speculative activity. In this sense interventions have provided leading indications about the future course of monetary policy. A comparison of the two episodes of exchange market shows that RBI's response to speculative attacks has followed a common pattern. While the exchange rate regime in India can be characterised as highly managed, this needs to be viewed against the transitional phase in the evolution of the regime and the development of the markets.

The second paper of the volume titled "A Case for Risk Based Deposit Insurance System in India" by D. Ajit, examines the deposit and credit insurance system in India in the light of experiences of other countries-both developed and developing. The paper examines a wide variety of issues related to Deposit Insurance System including flat fee and risk based insurance system for banks/non-banks, ownership of deposit insurance agency, continuation of credit guarantee scheme etc. The study brings out issues involving the need to move towards market oriented regimes with no deposit insurance (as in New Zealand) or to a flat fee based insurance system for banks as also the question whether the system of risk-based deposit insurance should be extended to non-banking institutions. The study has pointed out that the introduction of a flat- fee based deposit insurance system in India could amplify the regulatory divergence and moral hazard problems. The study finds that the case for private insurance is weak in view of the limited ability of the private insurers to provide guarantee. The study makes the case for introduction of a risk based deposit insurance scheme for NBFCS only after putting in place an effective regulatory and supervisory system for them.

The third article in this volume "Repo auction Formats, Bidders' Behaviour and Money Market Response in India" by Bhupal Singh and Sarat C. Dhal, illustrates the money market reaction to repo-auctions and examines whether such reaction is consistent with applied auction rules. The treasury securities repurchase agreement (repos) has evolved over a period as an important instrument for conducting monetary policy by central banks. Analysing the response of the money market to repo auctions, the study demonstrates that under discriminatory price repo

auctions, call money rates respond significantly to both low and high repo rates, but the degree of adjustment would be higher in the case of low rates than the high rates. There is, therefore, a lack of high degree of asymmetry in the way money market responds to the repo rates, as evidenced in the developed money markets. The empirical experiment with the fixed rate repo system, on the other hand, reveals a robust relationship between repo and money market rates. In this case, there is a high degree of asymmetry in the response of money market to high and low repo rates. This could indicate the effectiveness of repo system as a signaling mechanism for the money market rates. The results suggest that fixed rate repos provided a floor rate for money market and proved effective in stabilising the money market rates around this level.

The study titled "The Effective Cost of Small Savings" by M.S. Mohanty and Nishita Raje examines the nature and extent of the hidden cost of small savings and explores the possibility of reducing the implicit costs without adversely affecting their position in the budget. The note examines the tax status and presents the estimates of effective cost of small savings and finds that the effective cost of borrowing instruments of the Government is very sensitive to tax rate on interest income. The note observes that tax rebates on small savings schemes crate a large difference in costs compared to unrebated borrowing instruments. The study notes that if market related interest rates is offered on small savings, return on small savings would become sensitive to the expectations about the future changes in interest rates and thereby the inflation rate. The study suggests that the attractiveness of small savings instruments to households, pensioners and institutional investors could be enhanced if such instruments could be made to provide a guaranteed real return to investors, by indexing the instruments so as to provide them a hedge against future inflation.

The note titled "Financing of Power Projects in India : Problems and Prospects" by R.K. Jain discusses the various policy initiatives by the Government to open up the power sector to private investment. The note discusses a number of issues relating to the financing of the power sector. The study identifies four main approaches, viz, public ownership and operation, public ownership and private operation, private ownership and operation and community and user provision and examines their possible application in Indian states. The note observes that a lot needs to be done in the policy front to address the basic issues of power sector financing, including, pricing of electricity, transmission, and distribution (T&D) network, promotion, of external financing, sustainable debt-equity ratio etc.

The volume also contains a review article titled "Money and Finance through the Looking Glass" by Y.V. Reddy, the review being of the book "Indian Economy : Essays on Money and Finance" by Dr.C. Rangarajan. The article discusses the various facets of Dr.Rangarajan's contribution in the field of monetary management, financial sector reforms, external sector management and the debate on the role of state and markets in the Indian context. The review highlights the inter- linkages among the various strands of thoughts of Dr.Rangarajan. The review article explores the proposition that "money matters" which recurs in the book and observes that so long as the money demand function is stable and provides a predictable relationship among money, output and prices, it will serve as a sound foundation stone for monetary targeting. The review has highlighted the crucial significance of 'cautious sequencing of reforms' in the financial sector coupled with 'consistent and mutually reinforcing character of the measures taken' in boosting financial sector efficiency. The review also discusses the issue of external sector management,
